What to do if the toilet is overflowing

Turn the water off at the supply valve behind the bowl by turning it clockwise. That stops the flow and prevents more water from reaching the floor.

If the valve will not turn, lift the tank lid and hold the float up to stop the fill. Then call (704) 951-1635 if the clog or leak is beyond a plunger.

Common toilet problems and likely causes

Constantly running

A worn flapper or fill valve.

Weak flush

Clogged rim jets or low water level.

Rocking or leaking at base

A failed wax ring or loose flange.

Phantom refills

A flapper that leaks water into the bowl.

Clogs that return

A partial blockage further down the line.

Tank sweating or leaking

Failed bolts or condensation.

When to repair and when to replace the toilet

Many toilet problems are inexpensive repairs. A new flapper, fill valve or wax ring restores most toilets in good shape.

Replacement makes sense for a cracked bowl or tank, a toilet that clogs constantly or an older high-flow model that wastes water. Newer toilets use a lot less water per flush, which matters on a utility bill.

Installing a new toilet properly

A good installation depends on the flange. We check that it is secure, at the right height and in good shape, because a damaged flange causes leaks no matter how good the toilet is.

We set the toilet level, use a new wax or approved seal, tighten bolts evenly and test for leaks and flush performance. If the clog is deeper than the toilet, see drain cleaning.

Small habits that protect your toilet

Only toilet paper and human waste belong in the bowl. Flushable wipes, paper towels and cotton products cause blockages in the toilet and in the line beyond it.

Check the tank every few months by adding a few drops of food coloring. If the color reaches the bowl without flushing, the flapper is leaking and costing you water.

Water use and toilet choice

Modern toilets use far less water per flush than older ones, and a well-designed model flushes just as well. If your toilet is from the 1990s or earlier, replacing it can show up on the water bill.

Tell us about your household, your bathroom size and whether anyone needs a taller seat. We help you choose a toilet that works well, not just one that looks right on the shelf.

Code check

Code and standards for toilet repair and installation

A toilet installation follows the International Plumbing Code (IPC) as adopted in North Carolina, including flange, vent and water use requirements.

Replacing a toilet in the same location is a minor job, while adding one is regulated.
