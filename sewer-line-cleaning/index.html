What blocks a main sewer line

Main lines clog from grease, flushed wipes, roots and sometimes from a pipe that has sagged and caught debris. Which cause it is determines whether a simple cleaning is enough.

A clean line often runs fine for years, and a line with roots may be clear for six months and then clog again. We tell you which situation we found and whether a repair should come next, like sewer line repair.

Why root clogs are common near older Salisbury streets

Older neighborhoods have mature trees, and roots seek the moisture that leaks from sewer joints. Once inside, they grow into a mat that catches paper and grease.

Cleaning cuts the roots back, but it does not remove them from the joint. If you see them repeatedly, ask about a camera look and a lasting fix.

When cleaning is enough and when it is a warning sign

A one-time blockage from a flushed item or a grease slug is a cleaning problem and nothing more. A blockage that returns every few months is a warning that something in the pipe is catching debris.

Track how long the line stays clear after cleaning. If it is under a year, ask for a camera look, because the fix may be a small repair now that avoids a big one later.

Cleanouts: the small fitting that makes a big difference

A cleanout is a capped pipe that gives direct access to the sewer line. With one, a plumber can clear the line from outside the house and keep a mess out of your living space.

Many older homes have no accessible cleanout, so the work has to be done through a toilet or roof vent. Adding one is a modest project that pays off every time the line needs service. Ask us about it during your visit.

Code check

Code and standards for sewer line cleaning

Main line cleaning is maintenance, but the cleanout you use to reach the line is a code item under the International Plumbing Code (IPC) as adopted in North Carolina.

If a cleanout is missing or buried, adding one is regulated work and is worth doing at the same visit.
